    For teachers: what this pilot is, the model, and sources

    This is a trial of a different lesson design for Projectile motion, aimed at the idea RJ Physics tests show students miss: sideways motion and falling are independent.

    • One story. A World Food Programme airdrop in South Sudan. The question is where the crew lets go.
    • Students commit first. They place the release point, usually right over the target, and watch the bags land 360 m beyond it.
    • Linked pictures. The flashes slide down to the ground (equal gaps) and across to a wall (gaps of 1, 3, 5, 7). The same flight is watched from the ground and from the plane. The two velocity graphs give a box and a triangle, the same picture as the braking story.
    • Then they use it. A second flight with new numbers: fall time from the height, distance from the speed.

    Ways to use it. Project step 1 and take a class vote on the release point. Step 5 (which bag lands first) is a good turn-and-talk. Or assign it and collect “Tell it back” with Copy for Canvas. This page covers launches that start level; launches at an angle are not in it.

    The model. No air: 60 m/s and 176.4 m up, so the fall takes exactly 6.0 s and the bags travel 360 m. The second flight is 70 m/s and 122.5 m: 5.0 s and 350 m.

    • World Food Programme crews drop from about 200 m above the ground; the drop zone can be as small as a football pitch; a coordinator on the ground keeps everyone clear; 9,400 tons of food were airdropped in South Sudan in 2025 (WFP, 2026; WFP on airdrops). WFP calls airdrops a last resort.
    • The plane’s 60 m/s is my round number, not a WFP figure. 176.4 m was chosen so the fall is exactly 6 s.
    • Air matters for real bags. The last step lets students switch it on; that curve is a rough estimate for a 50 kg bag (drag grows with speed squared, top falling speed about 53 m/s), not a WFP number.
    • The plane and the land are drawn to scale. The bags are drawn larger than life so they can be seen.
