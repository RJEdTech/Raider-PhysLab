    For teachers: what this pilot is, the model, and sources

    This is a trial of a different lesson design for Circular motion and Gravity and orbits, the fall Unit 4 in Physics.

    • One story, seen from above. A car on a curved highway ramp hits ice. Then a test circle, then the Earth.
    • Students commit first, by drawing. They draw the car’s path on the ice. The page sorts the drawing into the three common ideas: follows the curve, flung outward, straight ahead.
    • Linked pictures. Velocity and the road’s push stay at right angles on the car. All the velocity arrows, moved to one point, make a second circle, and how fast its tip moves is the acceleration: that is where v² ÷ r comes from.
    • Two viewpoints. A phone on the dashboard, seen from above and from inside the car, for the “thrown outward” feeling.
    • Then gravity. The airdrop from the projectile story is thrown faster and faster until it never lands, and the Space Station’s numbers are checked against v² ÷ r.

    Ways to use it. Project step 1 and have students sketch the path on paper first. Step 4 (the phone) and step 8 (keep going faster) work well as turn-and-talks. Or assign it and collect “Tell it back” with Copy for Canvas.

    The model. A 1,500 kg car on a level 40 m circle at 10 m/s needs 2.5 m/s² toward the center (3.75 kN). Grip is 0.8, 0.5 and 0.1 times the car’s weight for dry, wet and ice. These are typical tire values, not measurements, and a real ramp is banked, which this ignores. When the car asks for more than the grip, it is drawn on the widest circle the grip allows. Real yellow curve signs are not this sum: they come from an engineering study of the speed drivers can take comfortably, well under the grip limit (FHWA, Procedures for Setting Advisory Speeds on Curves).

    • The orbit step uses Newton’s inverse-square gravity for the Earth (GM = 398,600 km³/s²), no air, from 400 km up. The Earth and that height are to scale; the tower is not real.
    • Space Station: about 400 km up, 7.66 km/s (about 5 miles every second), one lap in about 92 minutes, and crewed without a break since November 2, 2000 (NASA station facts). Gravity at that height, 8.7 m/s², is my calculation: 9.8 × (6,371 ÷ 6,771)².
    • The word “centrifugal” is not used. The page shows the phone going straight instead.

    Counting: this page counts anonymous page views and which step was reached, with no names and nothing a student types.
