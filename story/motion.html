    For teachers: what this pilot is, the model, and sources

    This is a trial of a different lesson design for Motion in one dimension. The physics is the same. Four things are different:

    • One story. A single stop, from the moment the driver sees a deer to the moment the car is still.
    • Students commit first. They place a flag before the car runs, and again at a higher speed.
    • Each picture is made from the one before it. The snapshots stack into the position graph. The gaps stand up as the velocity graph. The space under the velocity line becomes the formula.
    • Each term of the formula is shown on the road. v₀t is a ghost car that never brakes. ½at² is how far braking holds the real car back.

    Ways to use it. Project it and have the class vote on the flag before you lock it in. Or assign it and collect “Tell it back” with Copy for Canvas. The arrow keys move between steps, and the numbered buttons jump to any part.

    The model. Steady 20 m/s for 1 s, then a steady −5 m/s². Real braking is not perfectly steady, and real drivers vary.

    • Time before braking: the UK Highway Code assumes about 0.67 s; a 2017 review by TRL for the charity Brake put the average at 1.5 s (Brake, 2017). The story uses 1 s; the last part lets students change it.
    • Braking: the Highway Code’s dry-road braking distances (14 m at 30 mph, 38 m at 50 mph) work out to about 6.5 m/s², and it says to allow at least double the distance in the wet. “Dry”, “Damp” and “Wet” in the last part are 6.5, 5 and 3.3 m/s².
    • Texting: sending or reading a text takes a driver’s eyes off the road for about 5 seconds (NHTSA).
    • November is the peak month for wildlife crashes in Colorado (Colorado State Patrol).
    • Riccioli and Grimaldi’s drops from the Asinelli tower, reported in 1651, matched Galileo’s odd-number rule (Graney, translation of Riccioli, 2012). More in Faith and Physics.
